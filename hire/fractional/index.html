Fractional Research

A fractional UX researcher — defined capacity, real accountability

Your research needs are real, but they don't require someone full-time. A fractional UX researcher gives you experienced capability at a set number of hours per week or month — with clear expectations, fixed availability, and genuine integration into your team.

What fractional actually means

Reserved capacity, not leftover time

Fractional research is not a contractor who fits you in between other clients whenever possible. It's a researcher whose availability for you is defined and protected — a specific number of hours per week, regular rituals, clear communication norms, and the ability to plan ahead.

Not all researchers work well fractionally. It requires strong communication, comfort with context-switching, the ability to stay connected with a team when you're not there every day, and the discipline to deliver in constrained time. We match accordingly.

Set hours per week or month — defined, not ad hoc
Clear time-zone overlap for meetings, syncs, and real-time collaboration
Regular rituals — weekly or biweekly touchpoints built into the engagement
Researcher integration into Slack, Notion, Figma, or wherever the team works
Good fractional research requires

From the researcher

Discipline in managing time across clients, strong async communication, ability to stay oriented without constant visibility, and the capacity to hit the ground running every engagement.

From your team

Clarity on priorities, decisions documented, context shared without assuming availability, and respect for defined hours. Fractional works best when the team treats the researcher as a team member, not a vendor they can call anytime.

Is fractional right for you?

When fractional research works well

Research needs are consistent but not full-time

You run research regularly — user interviews, usability testing, concept validation — but 2–3 studies a month doesn't add up to 40 hours a week. Fractional gives you steady research muscle without full-time overhead.

You're building research culture gradually

Research isn't fully embedded in your product process yet. A fractional researcher can help establish practices, run studies, and advocate for research without requiring the organization to fully commit to a senior full-time hire at the outset.

Budget doesn't support a full-time placement

A strong senior UX researcher is expensive. Fractional access to that level of experience — at a fraction of the cost — is often a more rational allocation than a junior full-time hire with lower leverage.

Your research needs vary over time

Some sprints need research, some don't. Fractional engagements can sometimes be structured with flexibility to scale hours up or down within defined ranges — giving you research capacity that roughly tracks your actual needs.

You want to try research before committing

You've never had an embedded researcher. You're not sure how research would fit into your team's rhythm. Fractional is a lower-risk way to find out — and if it works, scale from there.

You're between hires

Your last researcher left. Hiring is underway but taking time. A fractional placement can keep research moving while the permanent search continues — and sometimes informs what you're really looking for in the next hire.

Making the call

Fractional vs. full-time: the honest version

Fractional research has real advantages — flexibility, access to senior experience at lower cost, lower commitment risk. It also has real constraints.

What fractional does well

A strong fractional researcher can handle the majority of what most product teams actually need from research. They can run studies, synthesize findings, advise on methodology, run usability sessions, build out research frameworks, and stay connected enough to the product to be genuinely useful. For teams with consistent but moderate research needs, fractional often delivers more than expected.

Where full-time has the edge

Full-time embedded researchers build institutional knowledge, sit in on more conversations, develop relationships with the full team over time, and can be more responsive to emerging research questions. When research is a daily practice rather than a periodic one, full-time wins. When you're building a research team, you'll need full-time leadership at some point.

The right question to ask

Not "which is better" but "what does your team actually need over the next 6–12 months?" If the answer is research that happens regularly, moves decisions forward, and produces genuine insight — fractional may be exactly right. If the answer is someone shaping your entire product development process from inside — you probably need full-time.

We'll tell you honestly which we think fits. We're not trying to fill every engagement with the most expensive structure.
